Corrugated Mailer or Shipping Carton: What to Specify

Quick answer
Specify a corrugated mailer or shipping carton as a complete packed system: contents, internal fit, box style, board construction, insert, sealing method and delivery route. Give each supplier the same drawing revision and acceptance criteria. Approve a filled sample before freezing the specification, and agree which transport checks it needs. A flute letter, FEFCO style code or attractive printed sample cannot establish shipping performance on its own.

Decide what the box must do
<p>Start the brief with one sentence: what is packed, how many units it contains, and which part of the journey this box must survive. A skincare subscription mailer going directly to a consumer has a different job from a master carton carrying retail cartons to a distributor. If the distributor repacks the goods, identify the protection that remains for the next journey.</p><p>Then state the appearance requirement. Is a scuffed outer surface acceptable if the contents arrive intact, or must the shipping box itself remain presentable as a gift? The second brief may need a different print finish, protective outer layer or acceptance limit. Translate the board description into something verifiable
<p>“Brown B-flute” leaves several purchasing decisions open. Ask for the proposed board combination and the performance measurements relevant to the job. Where a value is specified, identify its units, test method, sample conditioning and acceptance basis. Supplier grade names need a written definition before they can be compared across quotes.</p><p><a href="https://www.fefco.org/technical-information/fefco-testing-methods-recommendations">FEFCO lists separate tests</a> for board edgewise crush, bursting strength and water absorptiveness, as well as compression resistance of packaging. These describe different properties. A board test result should not be presented as proof that a filled box will survive the whole delivery route.</p><p>Engineering judgment: first identify the likely failure. A side wall folding under stacking load calls for a review of board, box geometry and load support. A loose glass jar needs restraint and protection; merely upgrading the outer board may leave the jar moving. A damaged printed face calls for a surface and handling review. Do not order “stronger board” until the failure mechanism is understood.</p><p>For the specific tradeoff between profiles, see <a href="/insights/e-flute-vs-b-flute-corrugated-boxes">E-flute versus B-flute corrugated boxes</a>. Keep that choice attached to the approved construction rather than treating a flute letter as a quality grade.</p>

Specify the insert and shipping seal together
<p>An insert should support the product where it can accept load, separate vulnerable parts and allow removal. Review the cap, pump, projecting corners and decorative surfaces, not just the widest product outline. A larger cavity may ease packing but permit movement; a tighter cavity may grip a label or load a fragile neck. Confirm the intended contact locations with the actual filled product.</p><p><strong>Illustrative example:</strong> a mailer contains a tall pump bottle and a squat glass jar. The bottle base is located, but its upper body can swing toward the jar when the pack is turned. Before adding board thickness, we would review upper restraint, separation and the available clearance around the pump. A separate support or revised cavity may be a candidate; its effectiveness still requires evaluation. This is a design scenario, not a reported MTT project or test.</p><p>Write down the dispatch closure, including tape type and width or adhesive-strip arrangement, application location and operator sequence. A tuck lock that holds on the packing bench still needs assessment as part of the sealed shipping system. If a return strip or tear strip is requested, check opening and resealing separately. Keep the shipping-label area clear of the intended opening feature. The <a href="/insights/custom-inserts-product-protection">insert selection guide</a> covers material and support options.</p>

Approve structure, appearance and transport separately
<p>A white structural sample can answer fit and assembly questions. It cannot establish production color, finish durability or all the behavior of a different final board. Record which materials and processes the sample represents. If a temporary insert, dummy product or substitute tape is used, list the questions still open.</p><ol><li><strong>Fit and packing:</strong> assemble the proposed box, pack every component, close it and remove the contents. Record snagging, distortion, unseated locks and product contact.</li><li><strong>Appearance:</strong> review a representative printed/finished sample against the artwork and agreed visual limits. Include creases, exposed edges, panel alignment and the label position.</li><li><strong>Transport:</strong> agree the route, procedure and acceptance criteria with the customer and testing provider. Evaluate the representative product, insert, box and closure together.</li><li><strong>Release:</strong> record the accepted drawing, board, artwork, sample IDs and remaining conditions. Identify the person responsible for closing each condition before production.</li></ol><p><a href="https://ista.org/test_procedures.php">ISTA distinguishes screening tests from general simulation tests</a>. Its <a href="https://ista.org/getting_started_with_design.php">selection guidance</a> calls for understanding the distribution environment, using representative samples and setting product-damage and package-degradation criteria before testing. Ask the testing provider to confirm the applicable current procedure and edition.</p><p>Define pass/fail in terms the buyer can inspect: for example, no leakage or breakage, acceptable product function, and an agreed limit for visible damage to the presentation surface. These are categories to agree, not universal allowances. A transport report must identify the tested configuration; it is not evidence of food-contact suitability, barrier performance or a supplier-wide certification. Requirements in those areas need their own applicable evidence.</p>

Compare quotes by scope, not just box price
<p>Require a base quotation against the same specification, with alternatives listed separately. Otherwise, a quote for a plain flat box can appear cheaper than one that includes printed interiors, assembled inserts and protective export packing. The unit prices describe different purchases.</p><div class="knowledge-table" tabindex="0" role="region" aria-label="Supplier quote comparison checklist; scroll horizontally on small screens"><table><caption>Resolve these differences before awarding the order</caption><thead><tr><th scope="col">Comparison item</th><th scope="col">Question for each supplier</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><th scope="row">Technical departures</th><td>Which board, drawing, finish or packing detail differs from the requested revision?</td></tr><tr><th scope="row">Included components</th><td>Are inserts, tape/strips, labels, assembly and export packing included or separate?</td></tr><tr><th scope="row">One-time charges</th><td>What is charged for samples, cutting tools, print setup and testing, and what is reusable?</td></tr><tr><th scope="row">Delivered basis</th><td>Are quantity, currency, trade term, named delivery point and freight/tax inclusions the same?</td></tr><tr><th scope="row">Schedule</th><td>When does the quoted lead time start, which approvals does it depend on, and is transit included?</td></tr><tr><th scope="row">Approval evidence</th><td>Which sample and test commitments are included, and what remains to be confirmed?</td></tr></tbody></table></div><p>Compare packed outer dimensions as well as supply volume. <a href="https://www.fedex.com/en-ca/resources-tools/dimensional-weight.html">FedEx Canada explains that its dimensional-weight charges can exceed actual-weight charges</a>. A more elaborate mailer may replace a separate component, but it may also add folding steps. Keep these as measured or quoted project inputs, not assumed savings.</p>

Carry the approved revision into production and reorders
<p>Attach the approved specification to the purchase order and packing instructions. Keep a reference sample that identifies its revision and approval purpose. Incoming checks can then compare the supplied board description, dimensions, print, insert count and closure against the same record used by procurement.</p><p>Reopen the relevant approval when the filled product weight, product shape, quantity per box, board combination, insert, closure, finish or distribution route changes. Record the proposed change and ask the responsible engineer or test provider which checks must be repeated. A supplier should not silently substitute a similar-looking board under the old sample approval.</p><p>As a China Packaging Development &amp; Manufacturing Partner, MTT can help turn the brief into structural proposals, samples and production-coordination requirements. The useful next step is a reviewable packed specification, with unconfirmed assumptions visible.</p>

What if I do not know the board grade for my shipping box?
Send the filled product weight, packing arrangement, route and handling requirements. Ask the supplier to propose a board construction and verification plan, identifying assumptions. Approve the resulting sample and specification before treating the proposed grade as fixed.

Can a plain white sample approve a printed corrugated mailer?
It can approve the structural features it accurately represents, such as fit and assembly. Keep color, print registration, finish behavior and any material differences open until a representative finished sample has been reviewed. State the scope of each approval in writing.

Does a FEFCO code or an ECT value replace a transport test?
No. A FEFCO code identifies a design; ECT describes board edge-crush performance under a specified method. Neither evaluates the complete filled pack through its intended route. Agree with the customer and testing provider what verification that configuration needs.

Send product photos, filled weights and dimensions, quantity per box, order quantity and the delivery route, plus any existing drawing or failed pack. MTT can review the corrugated specification, identify missing decisions and propose a sampling scope.
